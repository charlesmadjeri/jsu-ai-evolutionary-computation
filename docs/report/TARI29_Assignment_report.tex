\section{Traveling Salesman Problem}

•Given a set of cities \(C=\{c_1,c_2,\dots,c_n\}\) with coordinates, and a distance function \(d(\cdot,\cdot)\), the objective is to find a permutation \(\pi\) of the city indices that minimizes the total path length for the open (path) TSP:
\[
L(\pi)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} d\bigl(\pi(k),\pi(k+1)\bigr).
\]

We use the Manhattan distance between two cities \(c_i=(x_i,y_i)\) and \(c_j=(x_j,y_j)\):
\[
d_{\text{Manhattan}}(c_i,c_j)=|x_i-x_j|+|y_i-y_j|.
\]


• Three recent studies demonstrate the effectiveness of evolutionary algorithms in tackling the TSP. Bossek and Neumann introduce a Quality Diversity (QD) approach that evolves diverse TSP instances using a Map-Elites framework, enabling better algorithm selection by exploring the feature space and optimizing solver performance [1]. Saini et al. propose a hybrid quantum-classical evolutionary algorithm using Sort Gray Binary Encoding (SGBET) to ensure valid TSP tours and leverage quantum superposition for enhanced scalability on NISQ devices [3]. Meanwhile, da Silva Junior et al. conduct a comparative study of high-level hybridizations between eight metaheuristics and heuristics like k-OPT, SISR, and segment uncrossing, showing that combining evolutionary strategies with problem-specific heuristics significantly improves solution quality across various TSP instances [2]. 
These inspired hybrid approaches of the academic papers reinforced our choice for a hybrid of ‘exploration and exploitation’ in Order Crossover(OX) and Partially Mapped Crossover(PMX) as compared to say Cycle Crossover(CX) that leans towards exploitation and can be destructive to adjacency relationships.

• In the context of the TSP, maintaining the exact order or position of cities is less critical than preserving high-quality partial routes. The effectiveness of genetic algorithms in this domain largely stems from their ability to retain and recombine these beneficial substructures from parent solutions. Order Crossover (OX) is particularly well-suited for this task, as it maintains essential adjacency relationships while still enabling genetic diversity through recombination. This characteristic explains its consistent superiority over other crossover operators in TSP applications, regardless of whether the explicit sequence of cities is considered important. Thus motivating our particular implementation.


\section{Evolutionary Solver Algorithm}
To solve the Open Traveling Salesman Problem (Path TSP), we developed a tailored evolutionary algorithm. 
\subsection{Components of the Evolutionary Algorithm:}
\subsubsection{Representation}
Each individual in the population is encoded as a chromosome representing a permutation of city indices. This ensures that every candidate solution corresponds to a valid path visiting all cities exactly once.

\subsubsection{Initialization}
The initial population is generated randomly, with each individual representing a unique tour. The population size is a configurable parameter and was varied during experimentation to study its impact on performance.
\subsubsection{Evaluation}
Individuals are evaluated using a fitness function based on the total path distance. Since the problem is formulated as an Open TSP, the fitness is calculated as the sum of distances between consecutive cities in the tour, without including the return to the starting city. The goal is to minimize this path length.

\subsubsection{Selection}
We employed elite selection to retain the best-performing individuals across generations. A configurable elite rate determines the proportion of top individuals preserved directly into the next generation, ensuring that high-quality solutions are not lost. The displaced individuals are replaced by offspring generated by either OX or PMX from randomly selected parents from pool of elites and so on. 

\subsubsection{Crossover operators}
Two crossover operators were used to generate offspring:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Order Crossover (OX)}: This operator maintains the relative order and position of cities from the parents, helping preserve beneficial subsequences.
  \item \textbf{Partially Mapped Crossover (PMX)}: PMX retains absolute positions and mappings between parent chromosomes, which is useful for maintaining structural consistency.
\end{itemize}
Both operators were adapted to ensure that offspring remain valid permutations suitable for the Open TSP.

\subsubsection{Fitness function}
The fitness function is defined as the total path distance of the tour, excluding the return leg. This aligns with the Open TSP objective of minimizing the distance traveled from the first to the last city.
For an individual tour \(\pi\), the objective used is the path length
\[
L(\pi)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} d\bigl(\pi(k),\pi(k+1)\bigr),
\]
and selection favors smaller \(L(\pi)\).

\subsubsection{Replacement strategy}
The new generation replaces the old one, with elite individuals carried forward. This strategy balances exploration and exploitation, allowing the algorithm to refine solutions while preserving strong candidates.
\subsubsection{Stopping criteria}
The algorithm stops when any of the following conditions are met:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Time-based: Stops after a predefined number of seconds.
  \item Iteration-based: Terminates after a fixed number of generations.
  \item Improvement-based: Ends if no improvement is observed in the best fitness score over a set number of consecutive generations.
\end{itemize}
These stopping conditions were varied during experimentation to assess their influence on convergence and solution quality.

\subsubsection{Comparative Baseline}
To benchmark the evolutionary algorithm, we used a Greedy First Solver as a baseline. This heuristic constructs a tour by always selecting the nearest unvisited city, providing a fast but often suboptimal solution for comparison.

\subsubsection{Randomly generated datasets:}
We also created synthetic datasets with controlled variations in:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Number of cities
  \item Spatial distribution
  \item Coordinate ranges
\end{itemize}
These datasets enabled testing across diverse scenarios and helped evaluate the algorithm’s robustness.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Evolutionary Algorithm for Open TSP (path length as fitness)}
\begin{algorithmic}[0]
\REQUIRE Cities \(C=\{c_1,\dots,c_n\}\) with coordinates
\REQUIRE Population size \(N\), Elite proportion \(\rho\) (so \(E=\lceil \rho N\rceil\))
\REQUIRE Crossover rate \(p_c\)
\REQUIRE Crossover operator choice: OX or PMX
\REQUIRE Stopping criteria: time \(T\), generations \(G\), stagnation \(I\)
\ENSURE Best open tour \(\pi_{\text{best}}\)
\STATE Initialize population \(P_0\) with \(N\) random permutations (open tours)
\STATE Define fitness (cost) \(L(\pi)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} d(\pi(k),\pi(k+1))\) using Manhattan distance
\STATE Evaluate \(L(\pi)\) for all \(\pi\in P_0\)
\STATE \(\pi_{\text{best}}\gets\) individual in \(P_0\) with minimum \(L(\pi)\)
\STATE \(g\gets 0\), stagnation counter \(s\gets 0\)
\STATE Start timer
\WHILE{stopping criteria not met}
  \STATE Sort \(P_g\) by ascending \(L(\pi)\)
  \STATE Select top \(E\) individuals as \textbf{elites}: \(\mathcal{E} \gets \{\pi_1,\dots,\pi_E\}\)
  \STATE Remove non-elites: \(R \gets P_g \setminus \mathcal{E}\)
  \STATE \textbf{Fill} the population back to size \(N\) by generating offspring:
  \STATE \quad Let \(\text{num\_to\_create} \gets N - E\)
  \FOR{\(i=1\) to \(\text{num\_to\_create}\)}
    \STATE With probability \(p_c\) do:
      \STATE \quad Select two parents uniformly at random from \(\mathcal{E}\): \(p_a,p_b\)
      \IF{OX chosen} 
        \STATE \quad $offspring \gets \mathrm{OX}(p_a,p_b)$
      \ELSIF{PMX chosen}
        \STATE \quad $offspring \gets \mathrm{PMX}(p_a,p_b)$
      \ENDIF
    \STATE Otherwise (with probability \(1-p_c\)) do:
      \STATE \quad Copy a random elite: $offspring \gets \mathrm{copy}(\mathrm{select\_random}(\mathcal{E}))$
    \STATE Insert $offspring$ into new population $P_{g+1}$
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE Combine elites and created offspring: $P_{g+1} \gets \mathcal{E}\ \cup\ \{\text{all offspring}\}$
  \STATE Evaluate $L(\pi)$ for all $\pi\in P_{g+1}$
  \IF{any $\pi\in P_{g+1}$ has $L(\pi) < L(\pi_{\text{best}})$}
    \STATE \quad $\pi_{\text{best}}\gets$ best individual in $P_{g+1}$
    \STATE \quad $s\gets 0$
  \ELSE
    \STATE \quad $s\gets s+1$
  \ENDIF
  \STATE $g\gets g+1$
\ENDWHILE
\STATE \RETURN $\pi_{\text{best}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Solution representation }
Order based representation being the conceptual approach viz. each individual (chromosome) is a permutation of city indices or permutation encoding is used for our implementation. Permutation encoding is the most suitable solution representation for implementing the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) in evolutionary computation because it naturally models the problem as an ordered list of cities, directly reflecting the tour sequence. This encoding guarantees valid solutions—each city is visited exactly once—without requiring repair mechanisms, unlike adjacency-based or binary encodings. It also supports efficient fitness evaluation and integrates seamlessly with specialized crossover operators like Order Crossover (OX) and Partially Mapped Crossover (PMX), which are designed to preserve tour validity. Compared to random keys encoding, permutation encoding avoids the need for sorting and retains meaningful tour structure, making it both practical and evolution-friendly.


\subsection{Fitness function }

We used path length as the fitness function for our open TSP evolutionary algorithm, meaning each candidate tour is evaluated by the sum of distances between consecutive cities without returning to the start, and selection favors individuals with smaller total distances.
We use the open-path objective
\[
L(\pi)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} d(\pi(k),\pi(k+1)),
\]
evaluated with Manhattan distance. Selection favors individuals with lower \(L(\pi)\),  guiding crossover and selection to explore and exploit promising route segments while preserving diversity to avoid premature convergence.

\subsection{Experimental Procedure}

\subsubsection{Algorithm Configuration}

The evolutionary algorithm employs the following systematic design decisions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item{Selection Method}: Elite selection (fixed design choice ensuring the best individuals are preserved across generations)
  \item{Configurable Parameters}: The following parameters were varied during experimentation:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item{Population Size}
    \item{Stopping Criteria}
    \item{Crossover Rate}
    \item{Elite Rate}
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Execution Protocol}
The experiments executed the algorithm following this protocol:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Initialization}: For evolutionary algorithms, the initial population was generated using random permutations of city sequences.  
    \item \textbf{Independent Runs}: Each algorithm configuration was run at least 25 times with different random seeds to account for stochastic variation and ensure statistical validity.
    \item \textbf{Termination Criteria}: The algorithm employ multiple configurable stopping criteria:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Iteration-based}: Maximum number of generations/iterations
        \item \textbf{Time-based}: Maximum execution time limit
        \item \textbf{Improvement-based}: Termination when improvement becomes less than a specified threshold
    \end{itemize}
    The specific stopping criteria (or combination of criteria) has been fine tuned during the parameter search process.
    \item \textbf{Results exporting}: The results were exported in a CSV file for each parameter set.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Evaluation Data}

\subsubsection{Problem Instances}
The algorithm were evaluated on TSP instances of varying sizes to assess scalability and performance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Problem Sizes}: 76, 136, and 280 cities (EIL76, PR136, A280 datasets)
    \item \textbf{Number of Instances}: 3 benchmark instances from TSPLIB
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Data Generation}
The experimental evaluation employs benchmark datasets from TSPLIB:

\textbf{Benchmark Datasets}: Standard TSP instances (EIL76, PR136, A280) from TSPLIB. These benchmark problems are parsed and stored as arrays of tuples containing city coordinates $(x, y)$.

This standardized approach allows for direct comparison with existing literature and ensures reproducibility of results across different implementations.

\subsubsection{Distance Metric}
The distance metric used for calculating distances between cities is configurable as a parameter:

\textbf{Euclidean Distance}: 
\begin{equation}
d(i,j) = \sqrt{(x_i - x_j)^2 + (y_i - y_j)^2}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Manhattan Distance}: 
\begin{equation}
d(i,j) = |x_i - x_j| + |y_i - y_j|
\end{equation}

where $(x_i, y_i)$ and $(x_j, y_j)$ represent the coordinates of cities $i$ and $j$ respectively.

\subsubsection{Performance Metrics}
The following metrics were used to evaluate and compare algorithm performance:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Best Cost}: The minimum tour length found across all runs for each algorithm configuration
    
    \item \textbf{Average Cost}: The mean tour length across all independent runs, providing insight into algorithm consistency
    
    \item \textbf{Standard Deviation}: The variability of solution quality across runs, indicating algorithm robustness
    
    \item \textbf{Average Execution Time}: The mean computational time required for each algorithm to complete
        
    \item \textbf{Baseline Gap (Best)}: The percentage difference between the best solution found and the baseline algorithm's best solution, calculated as:
    \begin{equation}
    \frac{EA_{best} - Baseline_{best}}{Baseline_{best}} \times 100\%
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Baseline Gap (Average)}: The percentage difference between the average solution quality and the baseline algorithm's average solution quality, calculated as:
    \begin{equation}
    \frac{EA_{avg} - Baseline_{avg}}{Baseline_{avg}} \times 100\%
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Experiment Results Analysis}

From the experiments, we noticed a clear pattern: as the generation number increases, the cost generally goes down, meaning better solutions are found. This makes sense because more generations allow the algorithm to explore more possibilities and improve solutions over time.

For the smaller EIL76 dataset, the cost stays quite stable even with fewer generations, so running the algorithm for too long doesn’t always help much. For the medium-sized A280 dataset, we see some improvement when increasing generations, but it’s not very consistent. However, for the largest PR136 dataset, increasing the generation number is very important — with too few generations, the algorithm struggles and ends with high costs.

Overall, these results show that tuning the generation number is important, especially for bigger problems. Using too few generations can lead to poor solutions, while using enough generations helps find better answers.

\subsubsection{Analysis of Crossover Operator Performance}

The experimental results consistently demonstrate the superior performance of the Order Crossover (OX) when compared to the Partially-Mapped Crossover (PMX) across all tested datasets.

A key observation from the convergence plots is the rate of improvement. In each instance, the solutions generated by \textbf{OX converged towards better costs more rapidly} than those from PMX. The convergence curve for OX exhibits a steep initial improvement, whereas the improvement for PMX is slower and more gradual. 

Furthermore, the variance in solution quality for OX decreases significantly over time, as indicated by the narrowing gap between the best, average, and worst costs. This suggests a \textbf{stable convergence} towards a specific region of the solution space. In contrast, PMX maintains a higher degree of solution variance throughout the experiment, suggesting that it is \textbf{ less effective at consistently exploiting promising search areas}, leading to greater performance instability.


\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}


In this work, we studied how changing the generation number affects the performance of an evolutionary algorithm on three datasets of different sizes.

The results showed that for small problems like EIL76, the algorithm can find good solutions quickly, so a low generation number is often enough. For medium problems like A280, increasing generations sometimes helps but with mixed results. For larger problems like PR136, higher generation numbers are clearly needed to reach good solutions.

This means that when using evolutionary algorithms, it’s important to choose the generation limit based on the problem size. Too few generations can stop the algorithm before it finds good solutions, but too many may waste time without much gain.

For future work, we suggest looking into how generation number interacts with other parameters, like population size or mutation rates. Also, adaptive methods that adjust the generation number during the run could improve efficiency.
